# Pelt price and weight calibration

The deerskin post offer changes from **60 cents to 75 cents**. A passing trader pays **56 cents**, after the existing 75% offer is rounded down to whole cents. This is a period-anchored game estimate, not a recovered 1848 Oregon Trail tariff. Bison/rabbit offers and all skin weights remain provisional. This audit does not establish that rough-hide trading was available at every place represented by a game counter.

Recovery still displaces meat, consumes meals and takes time. Skins are not fully tanned robes or ready-to-wear leather.

## Evidence and the translation into rules

**Deer: a contemporary price range.** Charles Wilkes, *Narrative of the United States Exploring Expedition*, volume V (1845), printed page 158, describes California export trade, including approximately 3,000 elk/deer skins valued at 50 cents to $1 each. It does not distinguish species prices, processing grade, or the cash a visiting hunter would receive. The game uses the midpoint, 75 cents. Moving from an export valuation to a rough deerskin purchase offer is our inference. There is no additional supposedly historical processing discount hidden in that calculation. [Original page scan](https://geology.19thcenturyscience.org/books/1845-Wilkes-Expedition/Vol-V/PDFpages/1845-Wilkes-Expedition-V-0158.pdf).

An 1848 Osage report was also identified through a later historical citation, but its original valuation passage was not independently inspected. It is not counted as verified primary corroboration for this change.

**Bison: distinguish the product and stage of trade.** NPS's Bent's Fort warehouse account describes purchase in trade goods at 25 cents and subsequent sale at $3-$6. Those are different stages of exchange; the short account does not provide enough detail to translate its barter valuation into a visiting emigrant's cash offer. Its 100 lb bales of 8-10 robes imply about 10-12.5 lb per robe before allowing for packing, not a measured weight for our rough dried hide. Neither observation validates the current $1.50 or 12 lb. Both remain estimates. [NPS warehouse account](https://www.nps.gov/media/photo/gallery-item.htm?gid=59FA9313-155D-451F-6778F7BDA01BB077&id=59fd0bf1-155d-451f-67d9-ec1a281addc3).

NPS's processing account uses 1846-47 observations to describe repeated softening work over several days. It distinguishes the earlier robe trade from the later industrial raw-hide trade. Consequently, we retain the rough-skin description and do not award a finished robe after one camp day. This does not prove that any rough skin would find a willing buyer. Season, sex, damage and preparation quality are still missing from the game's valuation. [NPS processing account](https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/beol_business-of-bison.htm).

**Rabbit and physical mass.** The search did not establish a suitable 1840s local rabbit-pelt cash price or wet/dry mass series for the three represented animals. Later commercial rabbit breeds, tanned skins, fully dressed buffalo robes and 1870s hide-hunting prices describe different products or markets. We have not substituted them as exact 1848 measurements. Retaining the existing figures is a provisional design decision, not historical confirmation. Actual retained mass would depend on size, hair, moisture and how thoroughly the skin was fleshed.

## Economic implications

At the opening staple price of 5 cents per food unit, a five-person filling-ration preparation day (15 units) consumes 75 cents of replacement food. A single deer skin now matches that illustrative meal cost at a post, before considering displaced meat, transport, ammunition from the hunt or the day's lost travel. One rabbit skin does not. Several skins can share the same preparation day; buyer demand still limits sales. This arithmetic is not a prediction of average profit, and later food prices vary.

At a post the maximum existing deerskin buying allowance of 16 pays $12 instead of $9.60. No starting funds, food calories, hunting success, buyer demand, skin weights or preparation times were increased. This is a narrow price calibration, not evidence that farmer survival is now balanced.
